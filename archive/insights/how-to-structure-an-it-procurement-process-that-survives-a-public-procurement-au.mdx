## Why Public Sector IT Procurement in Ghana Is Won or Lost Before You Buy

Most adverse findings in **public sector IT procurement in Ghana** are not stories of corruption. They are stories of missing structure: a needs assessment nobody wrote down, an approval granted verbally, a score sheet that was never signed, a single-source decision taken without the authorisation the law requires. The purchase may have delivered working equipment at a fair price. What it cannot do, months later when the file is pulled, is prove that the process matched the rules. That gap between a reasonable buy and a defensible one is where audits are lost.

A Public Procurement Authority review does not re-run your judgement. It tests whether the process you followed matches the Public Procurement Act, 2003 (Act 663) as amended by the Public Procurement (Amendment) Act, 2016 (Act 914), and whether every decision along the way is evidenced in the file. IT buys attract scrutiny out of proportion to their value because they carry three audit triggers at once: bespoke technical specifications that are easy to write around one vendor, proprietary licensing that invites single-source justifications, and fast-moving technology that tempts entities to skip steps in the name of speed. What follows is a sequenced, document-first process that holds up when the PPA, or the Ghana Audit Service, comes to look.

## Map Your Spend to the Right Method and Threshold

The first decision sets up everything downstream: which procurement method applies. Act 663 ties the method, whether national or international competitive tendering, restricted tendering, request for quotations, or single-source, to the value of the requirement against the thresholds set in the Act and updated by the Authority. Get the bracket right and the rest of the process has a clear template. Get it wrong and you have built a compliant-looking file on the wrong foundation, which is itself a finding.

IT requirements get mis-bracketed more than most, usually by understating value. The most actively policed trap is splitting a large requirement into smaller purchases that each fall under a competitive threshold. Buying the network switches on one order, the firewalls on another, and the installation on a third, when they are one project serving one objective, is requirement splitting, and auditors test for it directly by examining timing, vendor, and budget lines. The defence is plain discipline: aggregate the true value of the requirement, including multi-site rollouts and phased deployments, and let that aggregate, not the first purchase order, choose the method.

> Single-source is a method you must earn, not a shortcut you may take. The justification belongs in the file before the order, not in your explanation after the audit.

Single-source procurement is legitimate for IT in specific circumstances: proprietary software available from only one source, or equipment that must interoperate with an installed base where compatibility genuinely constrains the field. But Act 663 does not let an entity reach that conclusion alone. Single-source use requires the prior approval of the Public Procurement Authority, and the file must hold the written justification and that approval. "It was easier to renew with the incumbent" is not a justification; demonstrable, documented, approved proprietary constraint is.

## Write Specifications That Are Competitive, Not Pre-Wired

An auditor reading your tender documents asks one question about the specification: could more than one capable supplier have won this? When the technical requirements read like a single vendor's datasheet, naming a specific model, a proprietary feature, or a brand without "or equivalent", the specification looks pre-wired and the competition that followed looks decorative. Keep requirements outcome-based and brand-neutral: specify the performance, capacity, uptime, and support you need, not the product you have already chosen.

That discipline also makes evaluation honest, because outcome-based requirements translate into measurable, scorable criteria. Define the support SLA, the warranty period, the response time, and the in-country after-sales presence as weighted criteria before the tender opens, not as impressions formed after bids arrive. Criteria fixed in advance and published to bidders are defensible; criteria invented during evaluation to favour a preferred outcome are the opposite.

Ghana's operating realities belong in the specification as scored requirements, not afterthoughts. Power instability under the ECG and VRA grid makes standby and UPS provision, and equipment tolerance for unstable supply, legitimate technical needs you can specify and score. Foreign-exchange exposure and import lead times, against a cedi that moves, justify clear terms on pricing validity, delivery timelines, and cross-border warranty handling. In-country after-sales presence is a real requirement for systems that must stay running, not a preference. Crucially, the trail starts before the tender: the needs assessment that establishes what the entity actually requires, and the budget approval that funds it, must exist and be dated earlier than the documents they justify.

## Constitute and Document the Evaluation Committee Correctly

Act 663 builds in a separation of powers, and auditors test that it held. The entity tender committee and the tender evaluation panel exist to keep distinct hands on distinct decisions: those who specify the requirement should not be the only ones who evaluate it, those who evaluate should not be those who approve the award, and those who receive and accept the goods should be different again. When the same individual specifies, scores, recommends, and signs for delivery, the control is hollow regardless of how clean the outcome was.

Composition matters as much as separation. The evaluation panel needs genuine technical competence for IT requirements, an evaluator who can read a network architecture or a licensing model, alongside procurement and, where relevant, end-user representation. Independence is not assumed; it is declared. Every member should sign a conflict-of-interest declaration for the specific procurement, and that declaration goes in the file. An undeclared relationship between an evaluator and a bidder, surfaced later, can unwind an entire award.

The evaluation record is the artefact the audit lives on. Expect to produce signed score sheets marked against the published criteria, panel minutes, and a clear written basis for the award recommendation, all consistent with one another. The failure that recurs most often is an evaluation report whose narrative recommends a supplier the scores do not actually rank first. When the conclusion and the arithmetic disagree, the auditor need not allege bad faith; the inconsistency alone is the finding.

## Build the Documentation Trail Auditors Actually Pull

When the PPA reviews a procurement, it reconstructs the whole transaction from the file. The core sequence it expects to assemble runs: needs assessment and procurement plan; budget and committee approvals; the advertisement or invitation; the tender documents and any addenda; the bids received with their opening record; the evaluation report; the award decision and notice; the signed contract; and the delivery and acceptance records. A complete file tells one consistent story from need to commissioning. A file with gaps invites the auditor to imagine what filled them.

Two habits separate a clean file from a vulnerable one. The first is contemporaneous record-keeping: documents created when the decision was made, with dates that run in a logical order. Back-dating, or a cluster of documents all signed the week before an audit, is among the fastest routes to an adverse finding, because impossible date sequences are easy to spot and hard to explain. The second is handling the human edges of the process: clarifications issued to bidders, debriefs offered to unsuccessful suppliers, and any complaints received and how they were resolved. These records demonstrate a fair, reviewable process, and Act 663's own complaints and administrative-review provisions assume they exist.

None of this survives without a system. Adopt a standard file structure and index so every procurement is assembled the same way, set retention periods that meet the Authority's expectations, and store the file so it can be produced quickly and completely on request. Public sector IT procurement in Ghana is rarely a single-site affair, so apply the same indexing discipline across regional offices and phased deliveries; a record held only on a personal laptop in a branch office is, for audit purposes, a record you cannot produce. Apply the test the auditor will: if the PPA asked for this file tomorrow, could you hand over a complete, consistent, dated bundle without reconstructing anything?

## From One Clean Buy to a Procurement Function That Scales

Running one procurement to this standard is an achievement. Running every procurement to it is a capability, and that difference separates entities that pass audits routinely from those that scramble each time. Each clean file becomes a template for the next; each properly constituted panel teaches the people who sit on the following one. Over a few cycles, document-first procurement stops being a special effort and becomes how the entity buys, which is exactly the maturity the Authority's assessments are designed to reward.

Procurement discipline also connects upward into the broader government IT operating model. The same rigour that produces an auditable buy feeds asset lifecycle management, vendor and contract oversight, and continuity planning, because you cannot manage what you never recorded acquiring. A procurement file is not paperwork for the auditor; it is the first record in the life of an asset you will run, support, and eventually replace. ## Frequently asked questions

### Which IT procurement records does Ghana's PPA actually request during an audit?

The Authority reconstructs the whole transaction, so expect to produce the needs assessment and procurement plan, budget and committee approvals, the advertisement and tender documents with any addenda, the bids received and the opening record, the signed evaluation report and score sheets, the award decision and notice, the contract, and the delivery and acceptance records. It also looks for evidence of clarifications, debriefs, and any complaints handled. The file must be complete, internally consistent, and correctly dated.

### What procurement thresholds trigger competitive tender versus single-source for IT?

Act 663 ties the method to the aggregate value of the requirement against the thresholds set in the Act and updated by the Authority, escalating from request for quotations to national and then international competitive tendering as value rises. Single-source is not a value bracket but an exception permitted only in defined circumstances, such as genuine proprietary supply or essential interoperability, and only with the prior approval of the PPA. Aggregate the true requirement value, including multi-site and phased work, because splitting it to dodge a competitive threshold is a finding auditors look for directly.

### How should an IT evaluation committee be composed and documented under Act 663?

The evaluation panel should combine genuine IT technical competence with procurement and, where relevant, end-user representation, and it must be separate from the people who specified the requirement and those who approve the award. Every member should sign a conflict-of-interest declaration for that specific procurement, retained in the file. The panel's output, signed score sheets against the published criteria, minutes, and a written basis for award, must be consistent with one another and with the recommendation.

### What are the most common IT procurement findings that fail a PPA audit?

The recurring failures are requirement splitting to avoid a competitive threshold, single-source use without the required prior PPA approval, specifications written around one vendor so competition was effectively closed, and undeclared conflicts of interest on the evaluation panel. Document-trail failures are just as common: missing needs assessments or approvals, back-dated or inconsistently dated records, and an evaluation report whose recommendation the scores do not support. Most are failures of structure and evidence rather than of price or intent.
