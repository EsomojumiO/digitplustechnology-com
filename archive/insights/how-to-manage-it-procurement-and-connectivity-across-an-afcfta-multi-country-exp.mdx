## Why Multi-Country IT Procurement in Africa Is a Different Discipline, Not a Bigger One

Expanding operations into a second or third African market changes the IT procurement problem in kind, not just in scale. Multi-country IT procurement in Africa is not single-country procurement repeated across more sites; it is a different discipline, because the moment a fleet crosses a border it picks up a new currency, a new import regime, a new data protection regulator, and a new connectivity reality. The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) is gradually lowering the tariff walls between these markets, but it does not harmonise data law, customs paperwork, or the price of a switch in local currency. IT leaders who treat an expansion as a copy and paste of the home-market playbook tend to discover the gaps the hard way: a standard laptop with a three-month lead time in one capital, a financial workload that a regulator says cannot leave the country, a vendor contract that turns out to be unenforceable across the border where the kit actually broke.

This article is a playbook for getting ahead of that. It covers the four decisions that travel badly across borders: how to read what AfCFTA actually changes, how to standardise hardware without ignoring local import and FX reality, how to structure vendor contracts that span jurisdictions, and how to make data-residency calls under different regulators. The goal is a procurement standard you set once and localise deliberately, rather than one you re-argue in every market.

## Separate the Tariff Question from the Regulation Question

The single most useful mental move at the start of an expansion is to stop treating AfCFTA as a general simplifier. It is a trade agreement. Its work is reducing tariffs and easing the movement of goods between member states over a phased timetable, and that phasing is uneven in practice from one corridor to the next. What it does not do is collapse the regulatory differences that actually govern your IT estate. Data protection law, sector rules for banking or healthcare, technical standards, and customs documentation all remain national competences.

So split the planning into two ledgers. On the tariff ledger, AfCFTA may, over time, lower the landed cost of moving qualifying hardware between participating markets, which is a genuine but gradual saving worth modelling rather than assuming. On the regulation ledger, nothing is harmonised: each new market brings its own regulator, its own lawful-basis expectations, and its own paperwork at the border. Conflating the two is how teams end up assuming a single compliance posture covers the whole expansion. It does not. Plan duty cost as a slope that may improve, and plan regulatory compliance as a fresh obligation in every jurisdiction.

## Standardising Hardware Without Ignoring Local Import and FX Reality

Standardisation is the largest lever in multi-country IT procurement in Africa, and the easiest to get wrong. The instinct is correct: a small, consistent set of device and infrastructure builds across markets gives you simpler support, shared spares logic, repeatable imaging, and a security baseline you can actually audit. The error is standardising on a specific model number and assuming it is buyable everywhere on the same terms. A configuration that is stocked and supported in Lagos may carry a long lead time in Nairobi or Accra, and its price moves independently against the naira, the cedi, the shilling, and the rand depending on where and when you buy.

The resolution is to define the standard as a specification rather than a SKU. Write the standard around what the business requires: a performance tier, a security and manageability baseline, a minimum warranty and support commitment, and end-of-life expectations. Then maintain a list of approved equivalents per market that meet that specification, so local sourcing can flex to what is genuinely available and competitively priced without breaking the estate-wide standard. Centralise the standard; localise the sourcing.

FX and import reality then sit inside that frame rather than fighting it. Price total cost of ownership in each market in local currency, build in the duty and clearance cost that applies today, and stagger large purchases where you can rather than committing the whole fleet at a single exchange rate. Warranty and RMA terms deserve particular attention across borders, because in-country service is often thin and a covered failure can still mean a multi-week outage; the discipline of [negotiating warranty and RMA terms that hold up across African borders](/insights/negotiating-hardware-warranty-and-rma-terms-that-hold-up-across-african-borders) belongs inside the hardware standard, not as an afterthought. Treating procurement as a managed capability rather than a series of one-off buys is the core of a coherent [IT procurement](/services/it-procurement) function across multiple markets.

## Structuring Vendor Contracts to Span Multiple Jurisdictions

The contract question is rarely framed clearly enough. The real choice is between a centralised master agreement, separate per-country contracts, and a hybrid of the two. A global or regional framework with a large vendor can give you consistent pricing, standards, and escalation, but a framework signed in one capital is only as useful as its ability to deliver hands-on service in the market where a device actually fails. Local integrators give you in-country presence, spares, and clearance know-how, but a patchwork of independent local contracts erodes the consistency standardisation was meant to buy.

> A vendor framework you cannot enforce in the market where the kit breaks is a logo on a slide, not a remedy. Contract for delivery where the failure happens, not where the deal was signed.

For most multi-country expansions the workable answer is a hybrid: a central framework that fixes the hardware specification, pricing logic, and security baseline, executed locally through partners who carry the delivery, warranty, and RMA obligations in each jurisdiction. Whichever structure you choose, write the cross-border mechanics in explicitly. Name the contracting entity in each market, state the currency of pricing and who carries FX risk, set Incoterms so liability for freight, duties, and clearance is unambiguous, and define the governing law and dispute route. State the service levels per site with named escalation contacts, because a regional support promise that routes through a queue offshore is not a commitment your branch can rely on.

## Making Data-Residency Decisions Under Differing Regulators

Data residency is where a multi-country estate most often drifts out of compliance quietly. Each market sets its own rules and names its own regulator. In Nigeria, personal data falls under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 and the oversight of the NDPC, with NITDA active in the wider digital governance space, while financial institutions also operate under the Central Bank's expectations for how customer and transaction data is handled. South Africa applies POPIA, and Kenya applies its Data Protection Act 2019 through its own data protection authority. The obligations rhyme but do not match, and some sectors carry localisation expectations that others do not.

The disciplined approach is to map data flows before you choose where systems and storage sit. Classify each significant data set by the regulators it touches and the sensitivity it carries, then design each class to satisfy the strictest applicable obligation rather than the most convenient one. Where a regulator expects data to remain in-country or expects you to demonstrate control and lawful basis over cross-border processing, treat that as a hard design input that shapes whether a workload lives in-country, in a governed regional facility, or centrally. The recurring theme across these regimes is accountability: being able to evidence where regulated data sits and how it is processed. Where you are unsure, treat the obligation as real and design for evidence, because a cross-border transfer that felt frictionless technically can still be a compliance breach.

## Connectivity and Continuity Across Borders

Procurement does not end at hardware and contracts; the connectivity between sites is part of the same decision. Traffic between two African markets does not always take the short geographic path, and intra-African routing can still depend on capacity that lands through undersea cables and exchanges outside the continent, which affects latency and resilience between your sites. Last-mile availability and pricing vary sharply by market and by city, and many branch sites remain single-homed on one provider.

So specify connectivity per site against a continuity standard rather than buying a single link and hoping. Decide which sites need a second, diverse path, which workloads must keep functioning during a link outage and therefore need local edge processing, and how each site rides out power transitions, because connectivity equipment is only as available as the grid, generator, and UPS behind it. Couple the power assumption into the connectivity specification explicitly. Then keep one consolidated view of monitoring across all markets, because a multi-country estate you cannot see centrally fails in ways no single contract will catch.

## Sequencing the Expansion Into a Repeatable Standard

The failure mode at the end is trying to stand up every market at once. Sequence it instead. Settle the hardware specification, the vendor framework, the data-residency map, and the connectivity baseline in your established market first, then pilot the full playbook in one new country before replicating it. Each market you add should be a configuration of the same standard, not a fresh improvisation, and each should feed back what local import, FX, and regulatory reality forced you to flex so the standard sharpens rather than fragments.

Codify the result as a procurement playbook the whole organisation works from: the hardware specification and approved per-market equivalents, the contracting structure and required cross-border clauses, the data classification and residency map, and the connectivity and continuity baseline. That playbook is the artefact worth producing, because it converts an expansion from a series of country-by-country scrambles into a standard you set once and apply deliberately.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does AfCFTA mean we can move IT hardware between African countries duty-free?

Not as a blanket rule today. AfCFTA is phasing down tariffs on qualifying intra-African trade over time, and implementation is uneven from one corridor to the next, so the saving is real but gradual rather than immediate or universal. Just as importantly, lower tariffs do not remove customs documentation, data protection obligations, or technical standards, which remain national. Model the duty benefit where it applies, but plan clearance and compliance as live obligations in every market.

### Should we sign one regional vendor contract or separate contracts per country?

For most expansions a hybrid works best: a central framework that fixes the hardware specification, pricing logic, and security baseline, delivered through local partners who carry warranty, RMA, and on-site service in each jurisdiction. A single regional contract gives consistency but can leave you without an enforceable remedy in the market where a device actually fails. Whichever route you take, write in the contracting entity, pricing currency and FX risk, Incoterms, governing law, and per-site service levels with named escalation contacts.

### How do we handle data residency when each country has its own regulator?

Map your data flows first, then classify each data set by the regulators it touches and design it to meet the strictest applicable obligation. Nigeria's NDPA 2023 under the NDPC, the Central Bank's expectations for financial data, South Africa's POPIA, and Kenya's Data Protection Act 2019 rhyme but differ, and some sectors carry localisation expectations. The common thread is accountability: be able to evidence where regulated data sits and how it is processed, and where you are unsure, treat the obligation as real and design for proof.

### Can we use one hardware standard across all our African sites?

Yes, provided you define the standard as a specification rather than a single model number. Write it around the performance tier, security and manageability baseline, and warranty and support terms the business requires, then maintain a list of approved equivalents per market so local sourcing can flex to what is genuinely available and competitively priced. This keeps support, spares, and security consistent across the estate while respecting the lead-time and FX differences between markets.
