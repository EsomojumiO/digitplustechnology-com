## The Compliance Problem Lives at the Branch, Not the Data Centre

Most Nigerian banks can produce a board-approved information security policy on demand. What they struggle to produce is evidence that the policy actually holds at the teller counter in Aba, the ATM closet in Port Harcourt, or the back-office PC in a Lagos branch that has not been reimaged in three years. The CBN cybersecurity framework is written at policy altitude, but examination increasingly tests how those risk-based expectations live at the branch edge, where controls are weakest and local IT is thinnest. Translating the framework into a defensible, evidenced branch baseline is the real work, and it is harder than writing the policy that promises it.

The failure modes are familiar to anyone who has walked a branch floor. Flat local networks where the CCTV recorder, the guest Wi-Fi, and the teller workstation share one broadcast domain. Endpoints with inconsistent patch states because nobody owns the build. Logging that exists in theory but cannot be pulled per branch when an examiner asks. This article is for two readers who must cooperate: the IT lead who has to implement these controls across dozens of sites, and the compliance lead who has to evidence them when the CBN comes to look.

## Reading the CBN Cybersecurity Framework as Branch Controls

The framework is organised around familiar security functions: governance, identification, protection, detection, and response. Rather than restate the document, the useful exercise is to ask what each function obliges you to do at a physical branch. Governance means a named owner for branch security configuration. Identification means an accurate, current asset inventory for every endpoint and network device on site. Protection covers segmentation and endpoint hardening. Detection means logs that reach a central store. Response means you can isolate a compromised branch endpoint without taking down the WAN.

The phrase that matters most in the framework is risk-based. Branches are not equal. A flagship branch processing high-value corporate transactions carries a different risk profile from a cash-light agency outlet, and your segmentation depth, monitoring intensity, and patch SLAs should scale accordingly. Applying uniform, expensive controls everywhere is not what risk-based means, and it wastes scarce budget that FX pressure has already tightened. Tier your branches, document the tiering logic, and let the tier drive the control intensity.

Keep CBN obligations distinct from adjacent ones. Customer personal data handled at the branch also falls under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 and the oversight of the NDPC. The two regimes overlap but are not the same regulator or the same evidence. Treat data protection controls as a parallel obligation, not a substitute, and avoid conflating CBN examination evidence with NDPA compliance records. One principle should drive every decision below: a control that produces no inspectable evidence is, for examination purposes, a control that does not exist.

## Segmenting the Branch Network

Segmentation is the single highest-leverage branch control, and it is where flat-network legacy hurts most. The minimum viable design separates traffic into distinct VLANs: teller and core-banking traffic, back-office workstations, ATM and self-service devices, guest or staff Wi-Fi, and physical-security systems such as CCTV and access control. These populations have no business talking to each other, and collapsing them into one segment turns a single compromised device into a branch-wide incident.

The goal beyond separation is east-west containment. A workstation infected through a phishing payload should not be able to reach the core-banking segment, and crucially it should not be able to traverse the WAN to reach another branch or head-office systems. Inter-VLAN traffic passes through a firewall or layer-3 policy that denies by default and permits only the specific flows each segment legitimately needs. Branch-to-HQ links should be encrypted and authenticated, not trusted simply because they ride a leased line.

> Segmentation is not a one-branch project. It is a template you apply identically to every site, so that the control you can describe is the control an examiner finds.

That repeatability is the real Nigerian challenge. With dozens of branches and minimal local IT, you cannot hand-build segmentation site by site. Standardise a reference design, push it through configuration management, and verify conformance centrally. One trade-off deserves honest attention: segmentation must degrade gracefully. When a leased line drops or the branch cycles onto generator power, essential branch operations should continue rather than fail closed in a way that halts the counter. Design for the outage you will certainly have, not only the steady state you wish you had. ## Setting an Endpoint Hardening Baseline

If segmentation contains the blast radius, endpoint hardening reduces the chance of ignition. A concrete branch baseline should specify: full-disk encryption on every device, application allow-listing so only approved software runs, removable media disabled where business policy permits, centrally managed EDR or anti-malware reporting to a console, removal of local administrator rights from standard users, and a hardened, documented operating-system build image. None of these is exotic. The discipline is in applying them uniformly and proving you did.

Power realities shape how this works in practice. Branch endpoints routinely cycle through generator and UPS transitions, and a hardening regime that assumes always-on connectivity will drift. Build for intermittence: policy should reapply and state should report on reconnection, so a workstation that rode out a four-hour outage re-checks in and confirms compliance rather than silently falling out of band.

Standard-build discipline is what keeps a teller PC in Port Harcourt identical to the approved image. That means a golden image, configuration management that enforces it, and drift detection that flags deviation before an examiner does. Finally, draw a hard line between managed, bank-owned endpoints and any third-party or vendor devices that touch the branch network. Vendor laptops and contractor devices should never sit on a trusted segment; place them on a restricted VLAN with explicit, logged access only to what they need.

## Logging, Patch Governance, and the Evidence Trail

Detection rests on logs you actually collect and centralise. At minimum, forward authentication events, privileged-access use, endpoint security alerts, and changes to network or segmentation policy to a central SIEM or log store. Local-only logs are worthless during an incident, because the first thing capable attackers do is clear them. Centralisation also makes per-branch evidence retrievable, which is precisely what examination demands.

Patch governance needs the same risk-based logic as everything else. Set patch SLAs by branch tier and vulnerability severity, run a test-then-deploy ring so updates are validated before they reach branch endpoints, and document how you handle legacy systems that cannot be patched. Unpatchable systems are a fact of Nigerian banking life; the answer is compensating controls (tighter segmentation, stricter access, enhanced monitoring) and a written record of the decision, not silence.

Examination-readiness is a design property, not a scramble. Define retention periods that meet regulatory expectations, synchronise time across all sites so logs correlate (skewed clocks make an incident timeline unprovable), and confirm you can produce evidence for a single named branch on request. Be honest about budget: FX pressure means you cannot deploy premium tooling uniformly. Concentrate logging depth and patch automation where transaction risk is highest, and document that prioritisation as a deliberate, risk-based choice rather than a gap you are hoping nobody notices.

## Operating the Controls: Governance, Roles, and Cadence

Controls decay without ownership. The workable model splits responsibility cleanly: a central security team sets and maintains the baselines, regional or branch IT executes and reports, and compliance validates and holds the evidence. Each deviation needs an escalation path with a named owner and a remediation clock tied to the branch's risk tier.

Cadence turns this from intention into habit. Run periodic configuration audits against the golden image, maintain an exception register for every approved deviation, and treat the register as a living document rather than a graveyard. The aim is that a CBN examination becomes a report-pull, not a fire drill. The artefact that makes this possible is a control matrix mapping each CBN expectation to its branch implementation and its evidence source. When an examiner asks how you meet a given expectation, you point to the row, the control, and the log or report that proves it.

Branch hardening is one layer of a broader CBN-aligned security posture, and it is most defensible when it is visibly part of that whole. If you are scoping a branch baseline now, the practical next step is an honest gap assessment: tier your branches, map your current controls against the framework, and find the rows in your matrix that have no evidence behind them. ## Frequently asked questions

### What does the CBN cybersecurity framework require at the branch level?

The framework is risk-based, so it does not prescribe a fixed branch checklist. In practice, examiners expect to see governance ownership, an accurate asset inventory, network segmentation, a documented endpoint hardening baseline, centralised logging, and patch governance, all scaled to each branch's risk and transaction profile. The recurring test is whether each control produces evidence an examiner can inspect on demand.

### How should banks segment branch networks to meet CBN expectations?

Separate teller and core-banking traffic, back-office, ATM and self-service, guest Wi-Fi, and physical-security devices onto distinct VLANs, with default-deny policy controlling traffic between them. Enforce east-west containment so a compromised endpoint cannot reach core systems or other branches across the WAN, and encrypt branch-to-HQ links. Use one standardised, repeatable template across all sites so the design is consistent and verifiable.

### What endpoint hardening baseline satisfies CBN risk-based controls?

A defensible baseline includes full-disk encryption, application allow-listing, centrally managed EDR, removal of local administrator rights, disabled removable media where policy allows, and a hardened standard build image. Critically, the baseline must be enforced uniformly through a golden image and drift detection, and endpoints must reapply policy and report state after power or connectivity interruptions. Hardening you cannot prove is uniform will not satisfy examination.

### How do we evidence patch and logging governance during a CBN examination?

Maintain risk-tiered patch SLAs with a test-then-deploy ring and a written compensating-control record for systems that cannot be patched. Centralise authentication, privileged-access, and security logs to a SIEM with defined retention and synchronised time across sites, so you can produce per-branch evidence on request. The fastest path to a clean examination is a living control matrix that maps each CBN expectation to its implementation and its evidence source.
