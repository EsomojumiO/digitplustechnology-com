Most campus networks are not tested by daily traffic. They are tested for ninety minutes, twice a year, when a 500-seat hall fills with students sitting a computer-based test at the same moment. Sound campus Wi-Fi planning at a university in Nigeria has to design backwards from that moment, not from the gentle average of a normal Tuesday. The network that feels fast at 10 per cent occupancy is a different network from the one that has to carry thousands of concurrent, actively transmitting devices during an online examination, a results-release day, or a registration window. Getting from the first to the second is a question of capacity, and capacity is planned deliberately or not at all.

## Why exam season breaks coverage-based Wi-Fi

Most campus wireless is designed for coverage: walk the building, confirm there is signal everywhere, sign off. Coverage answers the question "can a device see the network?" It says nothing about the question that matters during an exam: "can several hundred devices in one room all transmit at once without the airtime collapsing?"

Those are different problems with different solutions. A single access point can blanket a large hall with a strong signal and still buckle when 300 laptops associate to it, because radio is a shared medium. Devices take turns to transmit, and as the number of clients on one radio rises, each client waits longer for its turn. Throughput per device falls, retries climb, and the symptom students report is not "no signal" but "the page is loading" and "my submission timed out."

Exam season concentrates every variable that coverage-based design ignores. The devices are densely packed, they are all active at once rather than idle in pockets, and the cost of failure is acute: a dropped session during a timed assessment is not an inconvenience, it is a disputed result. This is why a network that has performed adequately for years can fail on its most visible day. Nothing broke. It was never built for the load.

## Campus Wi-Fi planning starts with modelling device density

Capacity planning starts with a number you have to estimate honestly: how many devices will be associated and active in each space at peak. Count generously. A 400-capacity hall does not present 400 devices; many students carry a phone and a laptop, and a phone left in a pocket still associates, scans, and consumes airtime. A 400-seat hall can easily present 600 to 800 associations during a busy session.

Work through your real peak events rather than a generic figure. The spaces that matter are the large examination halls, lecture theatres used for computer-based tests, the library during revision weeks, and any auditorium used for registration or town-hall events. For each, estimate concurrent active devices and the kind of traffic they generate. A browser-based test platform behaves differently from a session streaming video, and your per-device throughput assumption should reflect the heaviest realistic use, not the lightest.

> Design for the worst ninety minutes of the year, not the average week. A network sized for the peak handles the ordinary day comfortably; a network sized for the average fails exactly when it is most visible.

The output of this stage is a per-room target: concurrent devices, throughput per device, and therefore the number of radios required. Buying decisions follow from that target. Buying before it is how institutions end up with expensive equipment that is still under-provisioned in the three rooms that count.

## Access point placement and channel design for high-density halls

High density is solved by putting more radios closer to the users and shrinking the area each one serves, not by turning up the power on a few. A common and counter-productive instinct is to maximise transmit power so one access point reaches the whole hall. In a dense room that makes things worse: every device hears every access point, co-channel interference rises, and the cells cannot be reused.

The better pattern for a large hall is several access points, each at lower power, each serving a defined zone of seats, with overlapping but controlled coverage. This is a job for a wireless design and a survey, not a floor-plan guess. Nigerian campus buildings make the survey non-negotiable: reinforced concrete, blockwork, and steel rebar attenuate and reflect signal far more than the plasterboard partitions much vendor design guidance assumes. A predictive design should be validated on site, because the building physics will not match the model.

Channel planning is the other half. The 5 GHz band offers many more non-overlapping channels than 2.4 GHz and should carry the load in dense spaces; 2.4 GHz is best treated as a fallback for older devices because its handful of usable channels interfere badly at density. Where the hardware supports 6 GHz, it adds valuable spectrum for capacity. Plan channel width deliberately: narrower channels yield more independent cells, which usually matters more in a packed hall than the headline speed of a wide channel.

## Sizing backhaul, switching, and the internet uplink

Wireless capacity is wasted if the wired path behind it is the bottleneck. Each high-density access point needs a wired uplink sized to the aggregate traffic it will carry, and the switches serving exam halls should have the port capacity and uplink headroom to carry every access point at once, not on average. Power over Ethernet has to be planned as deliberately as data: confirm the switches can deliver the required PoE standard to every access point simultaneously, including the newer access points that draw more power.

The internet uplink deserves separate scrutiny, because it behaves differently depending on where the exam platform lives. A locally hosted or on-campus assessment system keeps most traffic on the internal network and leans less on the external link. A cloud-hosted platform routes every student's session out through the campus uplink, and that link becomes the single point of failure for the entire examination. Size it for concurrent active sessions at peak, and treat redundancy as part of the design rather than an upgrade for later. Last-mile fibre cuts are a routine operational reality in Nigeria, so a diverse second path, ideally on a different carrier and a different physical route, is the difference between a degraded exam and a cancelled one.

None of this survives a power event without protection. The network core, the distribution switches, and the access layer feeding an exam hall all need UPS coverage with enough runtime to ride through a grid outage and a generator changeover, with generator priority for the rooms that carry the load. A campus that plans wireless capacity but leaves the switch closet on unprotected mains has simply moved the point of failure. ## Authentication and onboarding that survive a login stampede

Capacity is not only about throughput. The first thing several hundred students do when they enter a hall is connect, and if every device tries to authenticate in the same few minutes, the authentication path becomes the bottleneck before a single exam page loads. A captive portal that asks each student to enter credentials in a browser, or a single RADIUS server with no headroom, will queue, time out, and produce a wave of failed logins that looks like a network outage.

Design onboarding for the stampede. WPA2-Enterprise or WPA3-Enterprise with a properly resourced and ideally redundant RADIUS back end handles re-authentication far better than a portal, because returning devices reconnect without manual entry. Pre-enrolling devices, or onboarding students well before exam day rather than at the door, flattens the spike. Keep authentication infrastructure on protected power alongside the network core, because an authentication server that drops during a grid event takes the whole hall offline regardless of how well the radios were planned.

Segmentation supports both performance and compliance. Examination traffic, general student access, staff and administrative systems, and campus IoT such as CCTV and access control should sit on separate SSIDs and VLANs. This isolates the exam network from contention and contains risk. It also matters for data protection: student records and assessment data fall under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 and the oversight of the NDPC, so the access controls, logging, and segmentation around examination and administrative systems should reflect those obligations rather than be bolted on afterwards.

## Validating capacity and operating through peak events

A design is a hypothesis until it is tested under something like real load. Post-installation validation should go beyond a coverage walk with a signal meter. Load testing that simulates hundreds of associated, actively transmitting clients in a hall reveals the airtime and backhaul limits that a coverage survey cannot. Where a full simulation is impractical, a controlled mock examination with real devices in the actual room is a reasonable substitute and surfaces most problems while there is still time to fix them.

Operating through peak events is a discipline of its own. The network team should watch the exam halls live during the event, with visibility of client counts, channel utilisation, and authentication success rates, so that a developing problem is caught in the room rather than reported afterwards. For an institution running examinations across multiple campuses, in Abuja, Lagos, and Port Harcourt for example, this monitoring and response capability has to exist at each site, because a centralised team cannot physically attend three halls at once. A short post-event review, capturing what saturated and what held, turns each peak into evidence for the next capacity decision. Building this operating capability alongside the academic systems is part of a coherent [education IT strategy](/industries/education) rather than a separate afterthought.

The through-line is simple. Coverage gets you a signal everywhere; capacity gets you a network that works when everyone uses it at once. For a university, the second is the one that protects the integrity of an examination, and it is the one that has to be planned, modelled, and validated before the hall fills. If you are assessing whether your current campus network would hold up under exam-season load, the place to start is an honest density model and a site survey of the rooms that carry the peak. ## Frequently asked questions

### How many access points does a large examination hall actually need?

There is no single number; it follows from the density model. Estimate the concurrent active devices at peak, decide on a realistic throughput per device for your exam platform, and let those figures determine how many radios are required and how the hall is divided into cells. A 400-seat hall presenting 600 to 800 associations typically needs several access points at controlled power serving defined zones, not one or two high-power units, and the exact count should be confirmed by a site survey.

### Is coverage testing enough to confirm the network is ready for exams?

No. A coverage survey confirms that devices can see the network, which is a different question from whether hundreds of devices can transmit at once without the airtime collapsing. Readiness has to be validated with load testing that simulates many active clients, or with a controlled mock examination using real devices in the actual hall, before relying on the network for a live timed assessment.

### What is the most common cause of Wi-Fi failure during a computer-based test?

In practice it is rarely the radios themselves. The frequent culprits are an under-resourced authentication path that cannot handle a simultaneous login surge, a wired uplink or internet link sized for average rather than peak load, and a single point of failure such as one RADIUS server, one carrier link, or unprotected switch power that fails during a grid event. Each of these can present to students as a network outage even when wireless coverage is fine.

### Do data protection rules affect how campus Wi-Fi should be designed?

Yes. Student records and examination data are personal data under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023, overseen by the NDPC, so the network should segment examination and administrative systems from general student and IoT traffic using separate SSIDs and VLANs, with appropriate access controls and logging. Designing this segmentation in from the start is more defensible and less costly than retrofitting it after an examination platform is already live.
