The cheapest IT quote is usually describing a different product.

Not always. But often enough that learning how to read an IT quote is worth
more than any negotiation tactic. A quote is the only document in the whole
purchase that the supplier writes before they have your money. Everything they
intend to deliver, and everything they intend to leave out, is already on the
page. You just have to know where to look.

This is a guide to reading one properly. It applies to any supplier, us
included.

## How to read an IT quote: could a stranger deliver it?

Hand the quote to a second supplier and ask them to price exactly what it
describes. If they cannot, because the description is loose enough to cover
five different machines, what you are holding is a price range with a
letterhead. Nobody is committed to anything.

"Core i7 laptop, 16GB RAM, 512GB SSD" fails that test. An eighth-generation i7
from 2018 and a fourteenth-generation i7 both fit those words. So does a
refurbished business unit and a new consumer one. The price difference between
them is large, and nothing on the page tells you which one is coming.

An honest line looks like this instead:

- **Manufacturer part number** (the SKU or product number printed on the box
  and the manufacturer's spec sheet), not a marketing name.
- **Condition:** new, factory sealed. If it is refurbished or open-box, the
  quote says so.
- **The configuration that matters:** processor model, RAM and whether a slot
  is free, storage type, screen, operating system edition.
- **Quantity and unit price**, then the extended price.

The part number is the line that does the work. With it you can look up the
exact configuration on the manufacturer's site, compare it against another
supplier's quote, and check the box on delivery. Without it you are comparing
adjectives.

When we quote, every hardware line carries the exact model, and the
specification is confirmed in writing before the order is placed. We do it
because without it nobody, including us, can check the delivery against the
promise.

## Warranty: how long, and who actually honours it

"1 year warranty" tells you almost nothing. Four questions turn it into
something you can rely on.

**Whose warranty is it?** A manufacturer warranty is honoured by the
manufacturer's service network. A "shop warranty" is honoured by the seller,
for as long as the seller exists and agrees with your reading of it. They are
different products.

**Will it be honoured in Nigeria?** Many manufacturers tie warranty service to
the country or region the unit was sold into. A laptop built and sold for
another market can arrive with cover that a service partner here will decline,
or will only honour after you ship it abroad. Most major manufacturers publish
a warranty lookup by serial number. Run the serial before you pay the balance,
not after the first fault.

**When does it start?** Cover usually starts from the manufacturer's ship date
or the registered purchase date. A unit that sat in a warehouse for a year can
arrive with most of its warranty already gone.

**What does a claim look like?** On-site next-business-day service and
return-to-depot are both called "warranty". The second one means a machine off
a desk for weeks. The quote should name the service level, and the length.

Our own practice is plain: warranties are registered in the client's name and
the documentation is handed over, not filed in our office. We do not supply
grey-market stock. If a warranty cannot be honoured in Nigeria, the unit does
not go on our quote. That is how our
[hardware supply](/services/hardware-supply) work is set up, and it is the
standard to hold any supplier to.

## Landed cost versus a number that will grow

A quote can be accurate and still incomplete. The question is where the price
stops.

**Ex-works** (or a price "ex-warehouse abroad") means the price of the goods
before freight, insurance, import duty, clearing and VAT. Those lines are real
and they are yours to pay. A buyer comparing an ex-works figure with a supplier
who quoted delivered to site is comparing two different things.

**Landed cost, delivered** means the goods are in your building, cleared, with
VAT shown on its own line. That is the only number you can put in a budget.

For imported IT hardware the gap between the two comes down to a few
variables. The exchange rate on the day the supplier buys the dollars sets
the base. Freight adds to it: air is faster and costs more per kilogram than
sea. Duty and clearing depend on the product category. That is why we
recommend comparing landed figures only, and asking any supplier who quotes
ex-works to restate it delivered. Our longer piece on
[total cost of ownership in a volatile naira environment](/insights/total-cost-of-ownership-for-enterprise-hardware-in-a-volatile-naira-environment)
covers what happens to that number over the life of the equipment.

### The validity window

Every honest naira quote for imported hardware has an expiry date, and it is
usually short. Anything from a few days to two weeks is normal. The price tracks the dollar, and a
supplier who promises to hold a figure for three months is either absorbing a
currency risk they have not priced, or planning to revisit the price once you
are committed.

Read a short validity window as information about what the price is made of.
The supplier is buying dollars on your behalf. Ask what happens if the rate moves
before payment. The answer should be written down.

## Delivery, installation and what is excluded

Two lines often go missing from cheap quotes: delivery and installation.

Delivery means to your site, at a stated lead time. "Ex-stock" and "four to six
weeks" are both legitimate answers. "Available" is not one.

Installation is where the hidden work lives. Unboxing, imaging, joining
machines to your network, migrating data, removing packaging, recording serial
numbers against desks. Either the quote covers that, or your own staff will do
it in the weeks after delivery. Both are valid choices. Only one is visible on
the page.

Then read the exclusions. A good quote has a short, specific list of what it
does not cover. A quote with no exclusions has not been thought through, and
the gaps will surface as variations.

## What the unusually cheap quote usually hides

When one quote comes in well below the others for the same description, the
difference has to come from somewhere. It is nearly always
one of these:

| What it hides | How to spot it |
| --- | --- |
| Grey-market stock | No part number, or a part number for another region. Warranty lookup shows a foreign ship-to country. |
| Refurbished sold as new | "New" with no mention of sealed boxes. Serial lookup shows a ship date years old. |
| Regional warranty | Warranty described as "international" with no service level. Ask which service centre in Nigeria will take the claim. |
| Missing accessories | Laptops with foreign plug adapters. Servers quoted diskless, without rails or power cords. Switches without the SFP modules that carry the uplinks. |
| No installation | No labour line at all. The setup becomes your problem. |
| Ex-works price | Freight, duty, clearing and VAT absent, or "excluded" in small print. |

Some of these are honest trade-offs. A refurbished business laptop at half the
price of a new one can be a sound purchase for a training room, if the quote
says refurbished and the warranty is real. A cheaper product is fine. A
cheaper product described as the expensive one is the problem.

## How to compare two quotes line by line

Put both quotes into one table. Columns for each supplier, a row for every
component. Then work down it:

1. **Match part numbers.** Where they differ, look up both. Note the real
   differences in processor generation, storage and service level.
2. **Normalise the price** to landed, delivered, VAT shown. Add the missing
   lines to the cheaper quote at a realistic cost.
3. **Compare warranty** as a service level, with who honours it.
4. **Add the labour** the cheaper quote leaves to you.
5. **Check validity dates.** A cheaper quote that expired last week is not a
   price.

Often the gap closes, or reverses. Sometimes it does not, and the cheaper
supplier is just cheaper. Then buy from them. That is the honest
outcome of the exercise, and it happens.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should every IT quote include?

Exact manufacturer part numbers, the condition of the goods, quantities and
unit prices, a landed and delivered total with VAT shown separately, the
warranty length and service level and who honours it, a delivery lead time,
whether installation is included, a list of exclusions, and a validity date.

### Why do IT quotes in Nigeria expire so quickly?

Because most enterprise hardware is imported and priced in US dollars, so the
naira price moves with the exchange rate. A validity window of a few days to
two weeks is normal. A long validity period on imported hardware means someone is carrying a
currency risk, and you should ask who.

### Is a refurbished laptop on a quote a red flag?

No. It is a red flag only when it is not labelled. Refurbished business
hardware with a real warranty is a reasonable choice for some roles. Ask for
the grade, the warranty terms and who services it.

### How do I check whether a warranty will be honoured in Nigeria?

Run the serial number through the manufacturer's warranty lookup and check the
start date and region. Then ask the supplier which service centre will accept a
claim and whether the service is on-site or return-to-depot. Get the answer in
writing before you pay the balance.
