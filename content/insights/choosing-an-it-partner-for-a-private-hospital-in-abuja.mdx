This guide is written by an IT supplier. Read it with that in mind.

We supply and support IT for a private hospital in Abuja, among other
clients, so we have an interest in how you choose an IT partner for a
private hospital. We have tried to write the
questions we would want a hospital to ask of anyone, including us. Where we
say what we do on a point, it is there so you can test it. Everything else
here applies to whoever you shortlist.

The people who usually make this call are a hospital administrator and a
medical director, sometimes with a board behind them. Neither is an IT
specialist, and neither should need to be. The questions below need no
technical background. They need a supplier to answer in writing.

## Integrity: is the equipment what it says it is?

### Is the stock genuine and new?

Ask for the serial numbers of the units being quoted, or at minimum a
commitment to supply them at delivery. Then check them yourself. The major
manufacturers run warranty lookup pages on their websites. Enter a serial and
you see the model and its warranty status, often with the country it was
sold for. A machine whose warranty started many months before your purchase sat
somewhere before it reached you, and some of its cover is already gone.

Region matters too. Some manufacturer warranties are serviced only in the
country of sale. A unit bought cheaply from another market can turn out to
have no usable cover in Nigeria.

What we do: we record every serial at delivery against the desk it goes to,
and register warranty in the hospital's name. You can check every one.

### Is the spec honest?

A quote that says "Core i5 desktop, 8GB, 256GB" is not a specification. It
describes a hundred different machines of different ages. Ask for the exact
manufacturer model and part number on every line. If the supplier will not
put a model number on the quote, they have not decided what they are going
to deliver.

Then ask the more revealing question. What would you cut? A supplier who
understands hospital workloads knows that most hospital management systems
run in a browser or a light client, and that an SSD matters more than a
faster processor. Their answer tells you whether they are specifying for
your desks or for their margin.

What we do: our proposals are line-itemed with exact models, and they say
where a cheaper option is enough.

### Are the warranty terms in writing?

Verbal warranty is no warranty. Ask for a written statement that covers:

- How long the cover lasts, and from which date.
- Who honours it: the manufacturer, the supplier or a third party.
- Whether repairs happen on site or the unit has to be taken somewhere.
- Where in Abuja a failed unit goes, and who carries it there.
- What voids it. Power surge damage is a common exclusion, which is one more
  reason for proper UPS and earthing at critical desks.

A supplier who is confident in the stock will put this on paper without
being chased.

## Structure: who is there when something fails?

### Who answers at 11pm?

Hospitals work at night. Ask the supplier to describe exactly what happens when the billing
printer stops at 11pm on a Saturday.

Listen for a named channel and a defined path: who picks up and when an
engineer is sent. Be wary if the answer is a personal
mobile number. One person's phone is a single point of failure. Ask what
happens when that person is ill or on leave.

### Is there a written scope and response target?

Any partner who intends to support you should be willing to state, in
writing, what they cover and how fast they respond and resolve by priority,
including out of hours. Our piece on
[what hospital IT support in Abuja should cover](/insights/it-support-for-private-hospitals-in-abuja)
lists the terms worth asking for.

What we do: our [managed services](/services/managed-services) work starts by
agreeing scope and response targets in writing, before any monitoring or
support goes live.

### Where are the engineers?

Remote support fixes most faults. Hardware needs a person. Ask where on-site
engineers are based, and how they reach your hospital at 5pm on a weekday.
A promise made from across the city deserves a second question.

### Will you own the documentation?

Ask who will hold the asset register, the warranty records, the admin
passwords and the build image after delivery. The answer should be: you
will, with the supplier holding a working copy. A supplier who keeps the
admin credentials to themselves has made you dependent, whether they meant
to or not.

## Behaviour: does the IT partner put the hospital first?

Integrity and structure can be checked on paper. Behaviour shows up in how a
supplier handles a conversation. We set out the general tests in
[what customer-first means in an IT company](/insights/what-customer-first-means-in-an-it-company).
Two matter most in a hospital, plus the one about leaving.

**Do they ever say no?** Ask for something you do not need. A high-end
workstation for reception, say. A partner will tell you plainly it is
unnecessary and explain why. A vendor will quote it.

**Do they ask about your hospital before quoting?** A useful first meeting
is mostly questions about your departments and what each desk does all day. A supplier who quotes before asking is selling stock.

**Do they make it easy to leave?** Ask what happens to your documentation if
you end the relationship. A partner who expects to keep your business
through good work has no reason to make leaving hard.

## References, and what to do with them

Ask for a reference you can phone, ideally another hospital or clinic. Many
suppliers, us included, do not publish client names, so do not treat an
empty logo wall as a warning on its own. Treat an inability to put you in
touch with anyone at all as one.

When you speak to a reference, skip the general satisfaction question. Ask
what happened the first time something broke, and whether the second order
was easier than the first. Those two answers tell you most of what you need.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should a hospital look for in an IT partner?

Start with stock you can trace and warranty terms in writing. Then look for
a defined out-of-hours support path and written response targets. The
best sign is a supplier who tells you when you are about to overspend.

### How can a hospital check that IT equipment is genuine?

Get the serial numbers and enter them on the manufacturer's warranty lookup
page. Check the warranty start date and the country of sale before you
accept delivery.

### Should a hospital use the same company for supply and support?

Not necessarily. One party owning the problem from purchase to repair is
simpler, and we make that case in
[one partner for IT procurement and support](/insights/one-partner-for-it-procurement-and-support).
It is not essential. What matters is that documentation and admin
credentials stay with the hospital, so either role can move to someone else.

### Is the cheapest quote a bad sign?

Not on its own. Compare quotes line by line on exact models and warranty
terms first. A cheaper quote for the same model and the same cover is just
cheaper. A cheaper quote with no model numbers is not comparable at all.
