Most organisations can show you a security awareness certificate and a click rate that fell from one year to the next, and conclude that their people are now harder to fool. Neither tells you much. A phishing awareness programme in Nigeria that runs once a year, measures only who clicked, and never changes what people actually do at their desks is a compliance artefact, not a control. The attackers running business email compromise against Nigerian finance teams are not deterred by a slide deck somebody watched in January.

The goal of a phishing-resilience programme is narrow and practical: make it more likely that a targeted employee notices something is wrong, hesitates, and reports it, and less likely that a single mistake turns into a fraudulent payment or a foothold in your network. That is a behavioural outcome, not a training-completion statistic. This article sets out how to run such a programme as a sustained operation, with the cadence, incentives, role-based focus, and metrics that move the outcome rather than the optics.

## Why click rate is the wrong scoreboard

Click rate, the percentage of simulated recipients who clicked a test link, is easy to measure, which is exactly why it dominates. It is also a weak proxy for the thing you care about. A low click rate can be produced by simulations that are too easy, by employees who have learned the look of your test templates, or by people who quietly delete anything suspicious without reporting it, which leaves your security team blind to live campaigns hitting other inboxes.

Worse, click rate frames the employee as the failure point. People click links for a living. The buyer who opens a supplier quotation, the HR officer who opens a CV, the accounts clerk who opens an invoice, are doing their jobs. A programme built to shame clickers produces fear and concealment, not resilience. The organisations that improve are the ones that shift the question from "who clicked" to "how quickly did we find out, and what happened next".

## From an annual event to a sustained phishing awareness programme in Nigeria

The single most important change is to stop treating awareness as an event. Behaviour decays. A skill practised once a year is not a skill. A resilient programme runs continuously and has four working parts that reinforce each other: realistic simulations on a steady cadence, role-based content, a reporting culture that is rewarded rather than punished, and a feedback loop that feeds live attack data back into the next round.

None of this requires a large internal team. It requires ownership, a defined cadence, and the discipline to act on what the data shows.

### Simulation cadence that builds a habit

Simulations should be frequent enough to keep recognition sharp and varied enough to avoid pattern-matching on your own templates. A monthly or every-other-month cadence works for most organisations, with difficulty and theme rotated rather than repeated. The point is not to catch people out; it is to give them low-stakes practice at the exact moment of decision, so the real thing feels familiar.

Vary the pretext deliberately. Mix credential-harvesting login pages, attachment lures, and, importantly, payment and procurement themes that mirror the fraud actually aimed at Nigerian organisations: a changed supplier bank account, an urgent request that appears to come from a director, a fake local purchase order (LPO) chasing payment. When someone interacts with a simulation, the most valuable moment is the immediate, calm, non-punitive teaching screen that explains the specific cues they missed. That just-in-time correction does more than any annual module.

### Role-based risk, not blanket content

Not every employee carries the same risk, and treating them identically wastes effort where it matters most. Map your phishing exposure to roles and weight the programme accordingly.

- **Finance and accounts payable** are the primary target for business email compromise, invoice fraud, and bank-detail changes. They need frequent, payment-themed simulations and a hard procedural rule: any change to supplier banking details is verified out of band, by a phone call to a known number, never by replying to the email.
- **Executives and their assistants** are targeted for authority-based fraud and are often exempted from training out of deference. That exemption is a gap. Whaling works precisely because the assistant believes the urgent instruction came from the principal.
- **HR, procurement, and customer-facing staff** open unsolicited attachments and links as a core part of the job and need content tuned to their workflow.
- **IT and privileged users** are high-value because their credentials unlock more. They warrant the most demanding simulations and the strongest enforcement of multi-factor authentication.

Role-based design also tells you where to put non-training controls. The most reliable defence against the changed-bank-account fraud is not awareness at all; it is a verification procedure that no single employee can bypass under pressure.

### Make reporting the rewarded behaviour

The metric that best predicts a contained incident is how fast and how often your people report suspicious messages. A reported phishing email is an early-warning signal that lets your team pull the same message from other inboxes before someone acts on it. An unreported one that was simply deleted protects one person and leaves everyone else exposed.

> The organisations that improve are not the ones with the lowest click rate. They are the ones where reporting a suspicious email is fast, frictionless, and quietly expected, so the security team learns about a live campaign in minutes rather than after the money has left.

To get there, make reporting effortless and make it pay. A one-click report button in the mail client beats asking people to forward to an inbox they cannot remember. Acknowledge every report, including the false alarms, because punishing a wrong guess teaches people to stop reporting. Recognise teams with strong reporting rates. And close the loop visibly: when a report helps stop a real campaign, tell the organisation, without naming or shaming anyone. People repeat behaviour that is seen to matter.

## The metrics that actually predict reduced compromise

Replace the single click-rate vanity number with a small set of measures that track the behaviour you want.

- **Report rate**: the share of simulated and real phishing that gets reported. This is the headline metric. Rising report rate is the clearest sign the programme is working.
- **Time to first report**: how quickly the first person flags a given campaign. Shorter is better, because it shortens the window in which others can be caught.
- **Repeat-susceptibility**: the proportion of people who interact across multiple simulations. A small recurring group is where targeted coaching and tighter technical controls should go.
- **Resilience by role**: report and interaction rates broken down for finance, executives, and privileged users specifically, so you can see whether your highest-risk groups are actually improving.
- **Real-incident outcomes**: near-misses caught, fraudulent payment attempts stopped before funds moved, and dwell time on any credential compromise. These are the outcomes the programme exists to change.

Report these to leadership in plain language. Under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023, accountability for personal-data security sits with the organisation's leadership and its data protection arrangements, so a board-level view of human-risk trends is a governance expectation, not a courtesy. Sector supervisors add their own weight: the Central Bank of Nigeria expects regulated institutions to run continuous staff awareness as part of their cybersecurity posture, and the Nigeria Data Protection Commission expects organisations to be able to show that staff are trained and that breaches are handled. Trend lines that show report rate climbing and high-risk-role susceptibility falling are exactly the evidence those expectations call for.

## Grounding the programme in the Nigerian threat reality

Generic phishing content does not prepare your people for the fraud actually aimed at them. The dominant local threats are financial and social: business email compromise, fake or altered LPOs chasing payment, invoice and supplier-bank-detail fraud, and authority-based requests impersonating directors. Your simulations and your verification rules should mirror these patterns directly.

The operating environment shapes execution too. Multi-site organisations spread across Abuja, Lagos, and Port Harcourt cannot rely on everyone sitting through the same in-person session, so the programme has to run and report centrally while reaching every location. Shared workstations and low-bandwidth or mobile-first access in some sites mean training must be short, lightweight, and accessible on a phone, not a one-hour video that nobody can stream. Staff rotation and contract hiring mean onboarding must enrol new joiners into the simulation cadence automatically, rather than depending on an annual cycle they may miss entirely. And foreign-exchange pressure on the cost of imported security tooling is a real constraint: when licence renewals are expensive, a well-run behavioural programme and a few firm procedural controls deliver disproportionate protection for the spend.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should we run phishing simulations?

Frequently enough to keep recognition sharp without causing fatigue. For most organisations a monthly or every-other-month cadence works, with the theme and difficulty rotated each time and payment or procurement lures included to mirror real local fraud. Frequency matters more than volume: steady, varied practice builds the habit of hesitating and reporting, while a single annual exercise decays long before the next one.

### Is click rate a useful measure of a phishing awareness programme in Nigeria?

On its own, no. Click rate is easy to measure but weakly linked to real risk, and a programme built around it tends to shame employees and suppress reporting. Track report rate and time to first report as your headline measures, broken down for high-risk roles such as finance and executives, and tie them to real outcomes like fraudulent payment attempts stopped before funds moved.

### Should senior executives be included in phishing simulations?

Yes, and they are often the most important group to include. Executives and their assistants are prime targets for authority-based fraud and business email compromise, precisely because requests appearing to come from a director carry weight. Exempting leadership out of deference creates the gap attackers exploit. Include them, and pair the awareness with an out-of-band verification rule for any payment or banking-detail request.

### How does a phishing-resilience programme support regulatory compliance?

A sustained programme produces the evidence supervisors expect. The Central Bank of Nigeria expects regulated institutions to run continuous staff awareness, the Nigeria Data Protection Commission expects organisations to show that staff are trained and that incidents are handled, and the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 places accountability for personal-data security with the organisation's leadership. Reporting human-risk trends, such as a rising report rate and falling susceptibility in high-risk roles, to your board gives you defensible evidence of an operating control rather than a one-off training record.
